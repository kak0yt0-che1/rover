## 1. Challenge statement (what the game must do)
Overview: build an interactive game where players design and simulate a complete space mission. Educational goal: make engineering trade-offs tangible, because they are often only treated theoretically.

Solution goals (single player or a team of up to 4):
1. Define the mission objective and requirements.
2. Design the spacecraft and scientific payload.
3. Select the launch vehicle and orbit.
4. Manage constraints on budget, mass, power, communications, data and duration.
5. Simulate the mission and evaluate the result.

A solid solution includes: a clear user interface; a mission configuration system; resource management mechanics; single-player and/or multiplayer support (up to 4); a simulation and an evaluation/scoring system.

The game should reward: mission performance, scientific value, efficient use of resources, quality of decisions, team collaboration.

Design considerations:
1. Game design and UX: accessible to different skill levels; single-player and cooperative modes; distinct team roles (planning, spacecraft, science, communications, resources); clear feedback on success or failure; ability to modify designs and rerun simulations; scoring with difficulty levels or scenarios.
2. Mission design: define the objective before technical choices; every decision has explicit consequences; multiple valid solutions; realistic constraints.
3. Spacecraft and payload: structure, power, batteries, communications, onboard computer, attitude control, instruments. Instruments have variable mass, power, cost, data volume and scientific value. Components can be simple or advanced depending on difficulty.
4. Orbit and launch: several orbits explained simply, with their effects on coverage, observation windows, communications and duration; launch vehicles with different capability and cost; a check that total mass fits the chosen orbit and vehicle.
5. Resource management: show SIX resources clearly: mass, power, budget, data, communications, time. Highlight which are critical for each scenario.
6. Collaboration: up to 4 participants with different responsibilities, shared final decisions, negotiation between competing priorities, results that depend on coordination.
7. Educational value: simple explanations of concepts, gradual introduction of complex topics (orbital altitude, payload capacity, data rate), short tutorials, visual indicators, optional resources, focus on WHY decisions affect performance.

Suggested additional features: multiple scenarios with different objectives; random events or unexpected constraints; realistic or simplified spacecraft models; interactive vehicle visualizations; real-time mission dashboard; leaderboard or mission ranking; final post-simulation report; saving, sharing and comparing designs; cooperative mode (up to 4); competitive mode between teams; sandbox mode without predefined constraints; post-mission analysis explaining success or failure.

Constraints: realistic limits on budget, mass, power, communications and lifetime; explicit consequences for every decision; no single "perfect solution"; mass, orbit and launch vehicle must be compatible.

Data: the statement lists no specific datasets (see the Resources tab on the hackathon page). Difficulty: accessible to beginners and intermediate players.

## 2. Judging criteria (five)
1. Influence (called "Impact" in the 2025 guide): how much good the project could do, in quality and quantity; big or small problem; how many people it could help.
2. Creativity: novel or untried approach, or only an incremental improvement.
3. Validity: scientifically sound; will it accomplish what it sets out to do; can it work in the real world.
4. Relevance: responds to its challenge; how complete, technically feasible and usable it is.
5. Presentation: how well the team communicated the challenge, the solution and why it matters. The guide asks teams to give this criterion equal attention to the other four.

Note: the guide gives guiding questions, not formal definitions; the lines above are paraphrased. Re-check wording if the site opens.

## 3. Global awards (ten)
Best Use of Science; Best Use of Data; Best Use of Technology; Galactic Impact; Best Mission Concept (most plausible concept and design); Most Inspirational; Best Storytelling; Global Connection; Art & Technology; Local Impact.

## 4. Eligibility
- Must respond to an official challenge (not "Create Your Own Challenge").
- Team 1 to 6 members, all registered and at the same Local Event.
- All required submission fields complete. Everything in English; a video demo may use another language only with English subtitles.
- Submit before 11:59 p.m. local time of the Local Event. Submission is final after "Submit for Judging".
- Confirm the work is original and every external resource is listed, no unlicensed copyrighted material.

## 5. Submission fields
Project Name; High-Level Project Summary (what you built, how it addresses the challenge, why it matters); Link to Project Demo; Link to Final Project (public website, repository or hosting); Detailed Project Description (what it does, how it works, benefits, goals, tools/languages/hardware/software); NASA Data (which NASA data was used and how it inspired or shaped the project; required for Global Award eligibility); Space Agency Partner & Other Data (all data, resources and tools, including code, text and images); Use of Artificial Intelligence (whether, which tools, how); Project Submission Verification.

## 6. Demo rules
- Video up to 30 seconds with English subtitles, OR up to 7 slides including the title slide, in English.
- Hosted on an external site with a public link. Files cannot be uploaded to the Space Apps site.
- Video structure tip: team intro; challenge and inspiration; project name and tagline; how it works (show it); future.
- Slides: team name, member names, challenge; readable fonts; few words; strong visuals of the project, the user experience, and how open agency data and technology made it possible.

## 7. AI disclosure rules
- AI use is allowed but must be stated clearly: where and how.
- AI-generated images and videos need a visible watermark.
- AI-generated audio, code and data must be acknowledged in the description and metadata.
- AI-generated content must not use or modify NASA branding or design elements.
